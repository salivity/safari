Safari Browser Market Share in 2008: Windows vs. macOS

This article examines the stark contrast in adoption rates for Apple’s Safari web browser between Windows and macOS (then known as Mac OS X) during the year 2008. While Safari enjoyed massive dominance as the default browser on its native Apple operating system, its expansion onto the Windows platform resulted in negligible market share. The following sections break down the specific market share data, the reasons behind the platform disparity, and how users reacted to Apple's cross-platform strategy.

The 2008 Market Share Numbers

In 2008, the global web browser market was heavily dominated by Microsoft’s Internet Explorer, which held roughly 70% to 75% of the total market, followed by Mozilla Firefox at around 18% to 20%. Apple’s Safari sat in a distant third place, starting the year with roughly 5.8% of the global browser market and growing to just over 7% by December 2008.

When breaking down Safari's total usage by operating system, the divide between macOS and Windows was massive:

  • Safari on macOS: On Apple’s own operating system, Safari was the undisputed leader. Roughly 70% to 80% of all Mac users used Safari as their primary browser, with the remaining Mac users largely opting for Firefox. Traffic from macOS accounted for over 95% of Safari's total global market share in 2008.
  • Safari on Windows: Despite Windows running on over 90% of the world's computers at the time, Safari on Windows failed to capture even 1% of the Windows browser market. Throughout 2008, Safari on Windows accounted for a mere 0.2% to 0.4% of total global internet traffic.

Why Safari Dominated on macOS

Safari’s high adoption rate on macOS in 2008 was driven by convenience and performance. Having replaced Internet Explorer for Mac as the default browser in 2003, Safari came pre-installed on every Mac.

Furthermore, Safari 3, which was the version active in 2008, was deeply integrated into Mac OS X Leopard. It utilized Apple's WebKit engine, offering Mac users fast page-loading speeds, native user interface elements, and seamless syncing with other Apple applications. Most Mac users simply had no compelling reason to switch to a third-party alternative.

Why Safari Struggled on Windows

Apple initially launched Safari for Windows in beta in June 2007, followed by a stable release of Safari 3.1 in March 2008. Steve Jobs hoped that introducing Windows users to Safari would showcase Apple’s software quality and help developers build web apps for the newly released iPhone. However, Windows users largely rejected the browser for several reasons:

  • Unfamiliar Design: Safari on Windows did not use standard Windows user interface conventions. Instead, it brought macOS styling—such as brushed metal textures and Apple’s specific font-rendering system—directly to the Windows desktop. Many Windows users found the text blurry and the interface out of place.
  • Aggressive Distribution: In early 2008, Apple used its "Apple Software Update" tool—which was installed on millions of Windows PCs via iTunes and QuickTime—to push Safari to Windows users. Safari was checked for installation by default. While this artificially inflated download numbers, it angered users and tech critics, who labeled the practice as borderline "barware" or spam. Most users who accidentally downloaded it never actually used it.
  • Fierce Competition: Windows users were already entrenched in Internet Explorer or actively migrating to Firefox, which offered a massive library of extensions that Safari lacked. Furthermore, Google launched its Chrome browser for Windows in September 2008. Chrome utilized the exact same WebKit rendering engine as Safari, but featured a native Windows look, superior speed, and massive backing from Google, instantly making Safari on Windows redundant.

Summary of the Disparity

Ultimately, 2008 proved that browser dominance on one operating system does not easily translate to another. On macOS, Safari thrived because it was the native, default choice designed specifically for that environment. On Windows, it was viewed as an invasive, poorly integrated novelty, resulting in a market share that barely registered on global analytics charts. Apple eventually discontinued Safari for Windows silently in 2012.
